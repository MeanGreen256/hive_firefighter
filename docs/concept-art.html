The direction I pitched is ground level — third-person, camera behind and slightly above your firefighter. Not top-down.

But top-down is a legitimate alternative and it changes almost everything downstream: how fire reads, how you aim a hose, how much art you need, and how big a team it takes. So here are all four viable cameras drawn out, plus the menu language that surrounds them.

Every frame below is hand-drawn in the target art style — flat-shaded low-poly, cool desaturated city, fire and apparatus as the only saturated things on screen.

My call: over-the-shoulder as the default, chase cam in the truck, first-person inside buildings. Three rigs, one world, one asset set. Isometric only wins if you'd rather build a crew-command sim than a firefighter sim — it's a genuinely good game, just a different one.

What to decide next

Three calls, in order

  1. Ground level or isometric. Everything else follows from this. Ground level is the more distinctive game and the harder build; isometric is the safer build and a genuinely different, more strategic game. Pick before any code.
  2. How deep the simulation goes. Arcade (spray the orange until it's gone) versus sim-lite (materials, ventilation, backdraft, the wrong agent making things worse). This sets the fire-cell data model, and retrofitting depth into an arcade model is painful.
  3. Open district or mission select. Whether you drive freely around a persistent city or pick a call from the dispatch map and load into it. Mission select is far cheaper and lets you ship the fire before you ship the city.
